The usual workaround is to make your own public-domain recording of the piece and have the generator sing over it. You can't feed the generator someone else's recording of Dukas.

Why not just upload a recording

Dukas's score (1897) is public domain, but nearly every orchestral recording of it is a copyrighted performance. Suno also screens uploads. One user who uploaded a Beethoven symphony got a message that the audio matched an existing recording, asking them to contact support if they own the rights. There are known ways to trick that filter, but don't use them. Besides the rights problem, a song about breaking safeguards can't be made by breaking one. org

Step 1: Make the instrumental from the score

  • Get the score from IMSLP. Scans of the original Durand edition are public domain. Typeset editions and MIDI files sometimes carry their own license, so log whichever you use.
  • Cut the roughly 11-minute piece down to about 3 minutes at the MIDI level, so the bar lines stay clean. Keep the moments the story needs:
    • the eerie opening
    • the bassoon "broom" theme
    • the build-up
    • the axe stroke and silence, which become the moment one bug report splits into two
    • the frenzy
    • the master's return
    • the four final chords
  • Render it with a free orchestral sample library. Log this step as a tool exception, the same way as your harmony workflow.

Step 2: Add the voice. Pick one route:

  1. Suno Add Vocals. It layers a custom vocal on an uploaded instrumental using lyrics you provide. The 'Audio Strength' slider in advanced options determines how much the new song adheres to the old instrumental, so set it high to keep Dukas intact. It's available exclusively to Pro and Premier users. A fresh render should pass the upload check. If it's flagged anyway, log the false positive and switch routes. Add Vocals +2
  2. ACE-Step 1.5, locally. It has no upload filter, and its output is exactly what your post-filter is built for. Its Lego mode adds an instrument stem into existing audio, and vocals are one of the stems it can add. Repaint regenerates a chosen time window while preserving the rest, so fixing one bad line is cheap. huggingfacechutes
  3. A vocal synth (ACE Studio or Synthesizer V) singing straight from the MIDI.

The catch that decides the route: Suno and ACE-Step write their own melody over your instrumental. If you want the singer to sing the actual bassoon theme, only route 3 guarantees it. My suggestion is a hybrid:

  • the vocal synth sings the broom theme, which is the hook everyone knows
  • a generator handles the narrator and the frantic verses, where an invented melody is fine

Write the lyrics to the theme's rhythm too. It's a fast 3/8, so roughly one syllable per eighth note.

Bonus bookend: if Suno's filter blocks your public-domain render, film it. A safeguard that refuses legitimate work while letting copies through fits a song about security tools.

Optical music recognition (OMR) can do this, but orchestral full scores are its hardest case. Plan on automating the bulk and correcting the rest.

Tools

  • Audiveris (free, open source). The current stable release is 5.10.2, from March 2026. It recognizes notation and exports MusicXML, and its built-in editor lets you fix mistakes on the page. This is the best free option, and it's AGPL, like the rest of your stack's open tooling.
  • Soundslice's scanner (paid). Users report it handles orchestral scores well, including an IMSLP Mozart symphony. One user's complaint: you have to assign each instrument and its transposition by hand, even when the score labels them. Scanning has a monthly page quota.
  • Other commercial options: PlayScore 2 claims good support for multi-voice and orchestral scores and exports MusicXML and MIDI. Flat, Newzik and ScoreConvert also export MusicXML. The newer research models (U-MusT, for example) are piano-only, so they won't help here.
  • MuseScore can't open a PDF directly. It sends the file to an online converter, limited to one PDF a day on the free tier.

Workflow

  1. Cut first. Only scan the bars you'll use, about 3 minutes of the 11. That means fewer pages to recognize and much less to correct.
  2. Prepare the images. Extract the scan's pages at native resolution (300–400 dpi), then deskew and crop. Don't upscale, since that adds blur that OMR misreads.
  3. Run two engines, such as Audiveris and Soundslice. Diff their MusicXML measure by measure with a script (music21 can do this). Where they agree, it's probably right; where they disagree, look. That's a few dozen spots instead of every note.
  4. Run the automatic sanity checks:
    • notes outside an instrument's range, which usually means a misread clef or transposition
    • bars whose durations don't add up
    • parts that go silent when the score shows they're playing
  5. Correct in MuseScore 4 (free), then export MIDI with one track per instrument for your DAW and sample library.

Where orchestral OMR usually goes wrong

  • Transposing instruments. Clarinets, horns and trumpets are written in other keys. A missed transposition makes a whole part play in the wrong key.
  • Clef changes, such as bassoon tenor clef and viola alto clef.
  • Shared staves. Two winds sharing a staff ("a2"), and divided string parts.
  • Tremolos, harp and percussion.
  • Systems that drop resting instruments. If the score leaves out instruments on some pages, the engine has to work out which staff is which.

For the production log: the OMR runs count as AI steps. Any correction made by hand is a human intervention under your rules, so either log it as one, or have the agent make the fixes from the flagged list.
